Sizing a settlement for a dust storm: one model alone, or two revising against each other

Solis sizes generation and storage over multi-year horizons. Tharsis simulates habitat demand hour by hour. On the left, Solis picks a capacity from a single demand number and stops. On the right, Solis picks, watches Tharsis run the storm against that choice, and revises. Those are two different settlement designs, one sized the way the partners work today and one sized with the two models talking to each other, and both are then put through the same storm in the same simulation. What differs is not the prediction, it is what got built, and how many of the 120 sols it lasts. Every number below is tagged with whether a partner stated it or this exercise assumed it.

The settlement being sized here is a phase 1 Mars habitat holding 1,000 residents. At 0.45 kW of continuous electrical demand each it draws roughly 450 kW around the clock, against 400 kW of installed fission surface power, so the balance has to come from solar arrays and from whatever those arrays can put into store. That power is not amenity. It runs air revitalisation and carbon dioxide removal, water recovery, heating against a night that falls well below freezing, lighting on a 24.66 hour sol, and the growing space. It is the reason anybody inside is alive.

Mars has planet-encircling dust storms. The research record puts the large events on the order of 120 sols, and while one is overhead the optical depth is deep enough that output from a solar array falls to a small fraction of its clear sky figure, in the worst of it a few percent. The load does not fall with it. If anything it rises, because the habitat loses passive solar gain and daylight has to be replaced electrically. NASA's own surface power planning for Mars carries a ride through case of the same 120 sol order, which is where the target on this page comes from.

So this model exists to ask two things. Whether a settlement sized the way such things are normally sized survives that storm at all, and whether sizing it with two models revising against each other produces a materially different design. The page runs the same settlement twice and puts both through the same storm.

The two models belong to different organisations. Solis sizes generating and storage capacity over multi-year horizons, which is the long view that picks an array, a battery bank and a hydrogen store. Tharsis simulates habitat demand hour by hour, which is the short view that knows when the load actually arrives. Below, two columns compare what each way of working builds. Both charts share one pair of axes, so the two columns are directly comparable. demand as the storm raises it, demand actually served after shedding, generation available, energy in store, battery plus hydrogen, the first hour a load goes unserved. The dashed line rising while the green one falls is the correlation, and it is the thing a single exchanged number cannot carry.

What the store above weighs

The two columns disagree most about the hydrogen store, and the sheddable slider moves it more than any other control. The split between load that can be switched off under stress and load that cannot is the number that sets it, and no partner declares where that split falls. This is what the half that cannot be switched off costs to land. Riding out a planet-encircling event of 120 sols is 2,959 hours, so every kilowatt of it needs 2,959 kWh in store. The multiplier is fixed. How many kilowatts it applies to is the number neither partner can compute alone.

Per resident demand is the one assumed constant chosen rather than researched. It is set where the design space the power partner declares actually closes, meaning the sizing rule lands inside the declared range for the array and the battery instead of running off the top of every one of them at once. A higher figure does not make the settlement fail more interestingly, it makes both columns identical at the ceiling and the comparison shows nothing.

What this does not establish

  • Nothing here carries uncertainty. Every number is one deterministic run of one set of assumptions, and a range is not the same object as a point.
  • Only electricity is modelled. Water, oxygen, buffer gas, thermal duty and carbon dioxide removal are the loads that actually end a settlement first, and none of them is here.
  • The dust state is one scalar with one shape. A real event varies over the site, over the spectrum and over the sol, and its optical depth is not a slider.
  • The reactor never turns down, no unit ever fails, and no maintenance is scheduled. The availability derate is a single assumed number standing in for a function nobody owns.
  • The demand uplift is the pivot of the whole comparison and it is assumed. Its direction is physically robust, because the habitat loses passive solar gain and daylight has to be replaced electrically, while suspended external work pushes the other way. Its magnitude is established by nothing. Set it to zero and the comparison collapses, which is the honest way to show what the argument rests on.
  • Per resident demand, the diurnal shape, the array capacity factor and the sheddable fraction were all chosen here. No partner publishes any of them.
  • This is a toy. It is an argument about what coupling changes, not a settlement design, and no configuration it prints should be built.
